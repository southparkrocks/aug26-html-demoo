Design History

National Parks have been areas of interest since Yellowstone's founding in 1872. Each National Park has their own unique features, that attract people from all around the world to want to explore the beauty of the American Landscape. What would make people interested in visiting National Parks? Each era of the ‘National Parks’ development saw a different Graphic Design trend. However, not all designs adhered to the trends of time. They greatly varied, due to a multitude of people and organizations handling the designs. Design had a big impact on getting people to visit the National Parks. Let’s explore some trends that led to the contemporary designs of National Parks.

Brochure Era

The Brochure Era was the first National Park design trend, during the 1910s. Photographers were hired by the Department of the Interior, to create black & white photographs for the park's brochures. Some other key features of this trend include center-aligned serif text over the photographs. The intention of this early trend was to gain support for the preservation of these natural lands by showcasing their beauty.

See America First

See America First was an advertising trend for National Parks from around the 1910s-1930s. This trend was heavily intertwined with railroad companies. Many Americans were only traveling to Europe because it was easier and more luxurious. In most of the West, transportation, lodging, and dining were hard to come by. Stephen T. Mather was the director of NPS from 1917-1929. He worked with railroad companies to promote Nation Parks using “See America First.” It was this idea that Western history and identity was a part of the national American culture. More rail lines, attractions, roads, hotels, and restaurants were built to get Americans to travel, to the parks. Key components of these designs were bold, colorful imagery and romantic language. Native American Imagery was also heavily utilized, which was ironic seeing how the building of all these “attractions” were pushing tribes out of their homes. This design trend was problematic, because it was just another way of promoting Manifest Destiny.

The Federal Art Project

During the Great Depression, The Federal Art Project (FAP), was signed into law in 1935 by President Franklin D. Roosevelt, as part of the New Deal. It ran from 1935-1939. FAP was created “to provide work relief for artists in various media – painters, sculptors, muralists and graphic artists, with various levels of experience.” FAP was a program under the Works Progress Administration (WPA). The posters designers made for National Parks During this era are most famously known as “WPA” posters, and their purpose was to incite public and civic engagement, as well as being promotional material for the NPS.

Dorothy Waught

Dorothy Waught is important to highlight because she was a key figure in American Modernist graphic design. She helped define the new visual language of federal design, with her bold, experimental works. Her impact is often overlooked because she was a woman in a male dominated field. “The federal government had never sponsored their own in-house poster campaigns, full stop—let alone had a solo female Modernist designer do such a campaign in what was a very male-dominated bureaucracy,” - Mark Resnick. Instead of the cut and dry showing of photographs with a caption, she pushed the boundaries by utilizing the lithograph to create bold color palettes, graphic shapes, and hand drawn unique letterforms. Her work depicted the broader idea of National Parks, and not just the individual parks and their features. Waught’s first even solo exhibition is currently on view at New York’s Poster House, “Blazing a Trail: Dorothy Waught’s National Parks Posters.”

Experimental Era

During the 1940s, 50s, and 60s, the NPS promotional material saw an era of experimentation, building on the design trends of the WPA posters during the 30s. They vary greatly depending on who made the work. Some key features of the designs during these decades include sans serif type, bold color, illustrations, and an altogether more artistic design approach.

Unigrid System

In 1973, the National Endowment for the Arts started the Federal Graphics Improvement Program, deciding that the NPS needed a cohesive visual identity. Massimo Vignelli was brought onto the scene, where he developed the Unigrid System in 1977; that is still used today. It features Vignelli’s signature simplicity: a title printed in reverse white Helvetica on a black bar stretches down the right side of brochures; maps echoed the same black bar up top.

Massimo Vignelli

Massimo Vignelli is an important person to highlight, because as mentioned above, he was responsible for the modern design system that national parks still use today. Aside from Vignelli’s contributions to the National Park System, he is most well-known for designing the modern-day subway system map, in New York City, in 1970. He was also responsible for popularizing the Helvetica font in corporate America. His approach to design was one of simplicity and functionality, reflected in his motto, “design is one.”
