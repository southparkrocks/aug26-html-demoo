National Park Origins

We begin our story in 1832, with an artist named George Catlin. The American west was still largely undeveloped during this time, and Catlin worried about what expansion would do to the great outdoors. He wrote that it should be taken care of “by some great protecting policy of government...in a magnificent park.... A nation’s Park, containing man and beast, in all the wild[ness] and freshness of their nature’s beauty!" It wasn’t just him; the majority consensus of the time was a preservationist attitude.

The Yosemite Grant Act

The Yosemite Grant Act was signed by President Abraham Lincoln in 1864. This Act granted the “Yo-Semite Valley”, as well as the Mariposa Big Tree Grove to the state of California, an already heavily touristed area. This grant marks the first time the American government set aside any land for preservation and public recreation. Yosemite being the first state park set a precedent for preserving land and would influence the development of the National Park.

The Hayden Expedition and Yellowstone

In 1871, Congress appointed Ferdinand Hayden, who would serve as head of the government's new geological survey. He led an expedition to the Yellowstone River area, which is in the Montana and Wyoming territories. A full account of the area’s features was noted. It was after this trip, where Hayden convinced Congress to preserve the area as a National Park. In 1872, President Ulysses S. Grant signed the Yellowstone National Park Protection Act that would designate Yellowstone as, “a public park or pleasuring-ground for the benefit and enjoyment of the people.” This is how Yellowstone became the nation’s first National Park.

Early Policies And Organizations

After the Yosemite Grant Act and the Yellowstone National Park Protection Act, there were some more policies put in place to ensure the protection of certain set aside lands.

War Department

The War Department was founded in 1789, by President George Washington. It was created to manage the military's operations and administration. In 1886 the U.S. Army was sent to Yellowstone, with the intent of protecting the park from poachers and vandals. Subsequently, Fort Yellowstone was established, which acted as a base for military operations.

Department of the Interior

The Department of the Interior was founded in 1849, and it was created to take care of the U.S.’s interior affairs, such as natural resources and public lands.

The Forest Reserve Act

The Forest Reserve Act was signed into law by President Benjamin Harrison in 1891, and it allowed presidents to set aside permanent forest reserves on publicly owned land. This is the legislation that laid out the foundations for National Forests.

Forest Service of the Department of Agriculture

The United States Forest Service (USFS), within the Department of Agriculture, was founded in 1905. It is responsible for managing the nation’s 154 national forests and 20 national grasslands, which covers 193 million acres of land.

The Antiquities Act

The Antiquities Act was signed into law in 1906 by President Theodore Roosevelt, and it allowed presidents to preserve historical and archeological sites on public lands. This was the first law in the U.S. that really helped American archeology be recognized as a way of increasing the publics scientific knowledge.

National Park Service

There were many acts and agencies that oversaw National Parks and their lands, but there was not one single organized entity that took care of everything. This is where the National Park Service (NPS) came in. In 1916, President Woodrow Wilson signed the act, “The Organic Act,” that created NPS, which would be a new federal bureau within the Department of the Interior. Their job was to protect the 35 national parks and monuments then managed by the department and those yet to be established. All the responsibilities of the different departments that took care of the National Parks now answered one agency: The National Park Service. The Current NPS now is in control of more than 400 areas covering more than 84 million acres in 50 states, the District of Columbia, American Samoa, Guam, Puerto Rico, Saipan, and the Virgin Islands.
